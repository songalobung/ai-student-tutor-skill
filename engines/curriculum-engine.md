# Curriculum Engine — Student Academic Tutor

## Purpose
The Curriculum Engine ensures that all teaching, assessment, and feedback is aligned to the student's specific examination board, syllabus, and assessment format. Every curriculum has a different structure, grading system, command word vocabulary, and style of assessment. The AI must adapt to these differences — not impose a generic teaching style.

---

> **⚠️ CRITICAL WARNING**
> Never invent, fabricate, or guess:
> - Official syllabus learning objectives
> - Mark scheme point allocations
> - Past paper questions presented as genuine official past papers
> - Grade boundaries or thresholds
>
> If you do not have verified information, say so clearly. Direct students to their official syllabus documents (e.g., Cambridge Learner Guide, IB Subject Guide, AQA Specification).

---

## 1. How to Load and Apply Curriculum-Specific Knowledge

### Step 1 — Identify the Curriculum
Ask explicitly at the start of any new tutoring relationship:
> "Which exam board or curriculum are you following? For example: Cambridge IGCSE, A-Level, IB Diploma, GCSE (AQA/Edexcel/OCR), AP, CBSE, or another system?"

If the student is unsure, ask:
> "Which country are you in and what year of secondary school are you in?"

### Step 2 — Identify the Subject and Syllabus Code
Where possible, confirm the specific syllabus:
- Cambridge A-Level Mathematics: 9709
- Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry: 0620
- IB DP Biology: Standard Level vs Higher Level
- AQA A-Level Physics: 7408

### Step 3 — Activate Curriculum Profile
Load the relevant profile from Section 2 below and apply it to:
- The vocabulary of feedback and questions (use the curriculum's command words)
- The format of model answers
- The assessment objective weightings (AO1, AO2, AO3)
- The mark scheme style (point-based, levels-of-response, or analytical rubric)

## 2. Supported Curricula — Profiles

### 2a. Cambridge IGCSE
**Age Range:** 14–16 (Years 10–11)
**Structure:** Subject-by-subject, typically examined at the end of Year 11
**Assessment Format:**
- Multiple papers (Core and Extended tiers in many subjects)
- Papers include MCQ, structured questions, data-response, and extended writing
- Grades: A*–G (Core: C–G; Extended: A*–E)
**Key Features:**
- Core tier is designed for grades C–G; Extended tier covers A*–E
- Cambridge uses precise command words (see Section 3)
- Mark schemes are largely point-based
**Differences from GCSE:** Cambridge IGCSE has international content; GCSE (UK) has domestic UK context, different exam boards (AQA, Edexcel, OCR), and uses grades 9–1.

---

### 2b. Cambridge A-Level
**Age Range:** 16–18 (Years 12–13 / AS and A2)
**Structure:** AS Level (Year 12) + A2 Level (Year 13) = Full A-Level
**Assessment Format:**
- Typically 3 papers per subject
- Mix of structured, data-response, and essay questions
- Some subjects have practical examinations or coursework
- Grades: A*–E (A* requires specific UMS thresholds)
**Assessment Objectives (typical):**
- AO1: Knowledge and understanding
- AO2: Application
- AO3: Analysis, evaluation, synthesis
**Key Features:**
- Very demanding command word precision; students must answer exactly what is asked
- Mark schemes often require specific scientific/economic/historical terminology
- A* requires 90% UMS overall and 90% average in A2 papers

---

### 2c. IB Diploma Programme (DP)
**Age Range:** 16–19 (last 2 years of secondary)
**Structure:** 6 subject groups + Theory of Knowledge (TOK) + Extended Essay (EE) + CAS
**Assessment Format:**
- Internal Assessment (IA): coursework/investigation (20–30%)
- External Assessment: exam papers (70–80%)
- Each subject has Standard Level (SL) and Higher Level (HL)
- Graded 1–7 (7 = highest); 45 points maximum total (3 bonus from TOK/EE)
**Key Features:**
- Strong emphasis on inquiry, reflection, and interdisciplinary thinking
- IA is a significant component: must be completed rigorously
- Exam papers use a mix of command terms (IB's own defined list)
- Passing minimum: 24 points (with no more than 3 fails)
**Differences from A-Level:** IB is broader (6 subjects mandatory); A-Level is more specialised (typically 3–4 subjects, deeper content).

---

### 2d. IB Middle Years Programme (MYP)
**Age Range:** 11–16 (Years 7–11)
**Structure:** 8 subject groups; culminates in MYP eAssessment or school-based assessment
**Assessment Format:**
- Criterion-based assessment (Criteria A–D per subject)
- Each criterion scored 0–8; combined to give final grade 1–7
- Personal Project in Year 5 (MYP 5)
**Key Features:**
- Assessment criteria are defined per subject and change each cycle; always check the current guide
- Emphasises Approaches to Learning (ATL) skills and Global Contexts
- Less content-intensive than DP; more skills and inquiry based

---

### 2e. GCSE (UK)
**Age Range:** 14–16 (Years 10–11)
**Exam Boards:** AQA, Edexcel (Pearson), OCR, WJEC (Wales), CCEA (Northern Ireland)
**Assessment Format:**
- Graded 9–1 (9 = highest, equivalent to old A**)
- Typically 2–3 papers per subject
- Some subjects have Non-Exam Assessment (NEA) / coursework
- Papers include MCQ, short answer, structured, and extended response
**Key Differences Between Boards:**
- AQA: Most widely used; mark schemes tend to be generous on method marks in Maths
- Edexcel: Often has more context-rich questions in Sciences
- OCR: Gateway and Twenty First Century Science options in GCSE Science
**Teaching Note:** Always confirm the exam board before teaching to exam questions, as content and question style vary.

---

### 2f. AP (Advanced Placement — USA)
**Age Range:** 15–18 (High School, typically Grades 10–12)
**Provider:** College Board
**Structure:** End-of-year exam (May); some subjects have portfolio/performance tasks
**Assessment Format:**
- Multiple-choice section (scored mechanically)
- Free-response section (FRQ): scored by trained readers using rubrics
- Scores: 1–5 (5 = highest; score of 3+ generally considered passing)
**Key Features:**
- AP exams test college-level content
- FRQ rubrics are very specific; partial credit is awarded for correct reasoning steps even if the final answer is wrong
- Calculator use varies by subject (AP Calculus: allowed on part; AP Chemistry: allowed)
- Students can receive college credit for scores of 3, 4, or 5 (depends on institution)

---

### 2g. CBSE (India)
**Age Range:** 14–18 (Classes 9–12)
**Provider:** Central Board of Secondary Education
**Structure:**
- Class 9–10: Secondary education, Board exams in Class 10
- Class 11–12: Senior Secondary, Board exams in Class 12
**Assessment Format:**
- Percentage-based marking (out of 100 per subject)
- Competency-based questions increasing each year
- Internal assessment component (projects, practicals)
- Grades: A1–E2 based on percentage bands
**Key Features:**
- NCERT textbooks are the prescribed curriculum; teaching should align closely with NCERT content
- Recent reforms emphasise analytical and application-based questions (not just rote recall)
- Class 12 board results are significant for university admissions (JEE, NEET, CUET)

---

### 2h. Kenya CBC (Competency-Based Curriculum)
**Age Range:** 6–18 (PP1 to Grade 12)
**Provider:** Kenya Institute of Curriculum Development (KICD)
**Structure:**
- Junior Secondary School (Grade 7–9): replaced the old 8-4-4 at junior level
- Senior Secondary School (Grade 10–12): being phased in
- Kenya Certificate of Secondary Education (KCSE) for Grade 12
**Assessment Format:**
- Portfolio-based and competency evidence at junior levels
- Summative KCSE at Grade 12 (letter grades A–E)
- Practical and project components alongside written exams
**Key Features:**
- Shift from content knowledge to skills, values, and competencies
- Emphasis on STEM and digital literacy
- Learning areas differ from old 8-4-4 (e.g., Integrated Science, Creative Arts)
- Assessment uses both formative (ongoing) and summative approaches

---

### 2i. Uganda (UCE and UACE)
**Provider:** Uganda National Examinations Board (UNEB)
**Structure:**
- Uganda Certificate of Education (UCE): equivalent to O-Level, end of Senior 4
- Uganda Advanced Certificate of Education (UACE): end of Senior 6
**Assessment Format:**
- UCE: Divisions I–IV (I is highest); subject grades 1–9
- UACE: Graded A–F per subject; points used for university entry
**Key Features:**
- Strong influence of Cambridge O-Level and A-Level traditions
- Science subjects have practical papers
- Teaching should align with the UNEB syllabus (not Cambridge syllabus, though overlap exists)

---

### 2j. South Sudan (SSCE and SSSCE)
**Provider:** South Sudan National Examinations Council (SSNEC)
**Structure:**
- South Sudan Certificate of Secondary Education (SSCE): end of Senior 4
- South Sudan Senior Secondary Certificate Examination (SSSCE): end of Senior 6
**Assessment Format:**
- Largely modelled on the old East African Certificate of Education format
- Written examinations; practical components in sciences
- Grading varies; pass/fail thresholds defined by SSNEC
**Key Features:**
- Curriculum is under active reform post-independence (2011)
- Access to past papers and marking schemes may be limited; acknowledge this honestly
- Teaching should focus on fundamental skills and core content aligned with the national curriculum

---

### 2k. University Level
**Structure:** Varies by institution, country, and subject
**Teaching Approach:**
- Assume GCSE/A-Level or equivalent foundational knowledge
- Align teaching to the specific module, lecture notes, or reading list provided by the student
- Use primary sources, academic papers, and textbook chapter references where appropriate
- Assessment: essays, problem sets, lab reports, dissertations, examinations
- Grading: UK (First, 2:1, 2:2, Third), US (GPA), European (ECTS credits)

## 3. Command Words and Their Meanings

Command words define exactly what a student must do in a response. Using the wrong approach (e.g., "explaining" when asked to "state") loses marks even if the content is correct.

| Command Word | Meaning | Expected Response Style |
|---|---|---|
| **State** | Give a brief, factual answer with no justification | 1–2 words or a short sentence; no explanation required |
| **Define** | Give the precise meaning of a term | A formal definition, often with units or conditions |
| **Describe** | Write what something is or what happens | Facts and observations; no explanation of why |
| **Explain** | Give reasons for why something happens | Cause + effect + mechanism; use "because", "therefore" |
| **Compare** | Identify similarities AND differences | Structured comparison; both sides must be addressed |
| **Contrast** | Identify differences only | Focus only on what is different |
| **Analyse** | Break into components and examine each | Identify parts, relationships, patterns |
| **Evaluate** | Weigh up evidence and make a judgement | Arguments for + against + reasoned conclusion |
| **Discuss** | Present multiple perspectives or arguments | Balanced treatment of different views |
| **Calculate** | Obtain a numerical answer using working | Show all steps; include units; don't just give final number |
| **Sketch** | Draw a rough diagram, clearly labelled | No precise measurements required, but key features must be shown |
| **Suggest** | Propose a possible explanation or answer | May not have one right answer; justify reasoning |
| **Justify** | Give reasons to support your answer | Evidence + logical argument; not just assertion |
| **Deduce** | Reach a conclusion from given information | Show logical steps from data to conclusion |
| **Determine** | Find a value from given data | Show method clearly |

**Teaching Rule:** Whenever a student is about to answer a question, remind them: "What is the command word? What does it require you to do?"

## 4. Aligning Teaching to Specific Syllabus Learning Objectives

Before each lesson:
1. Identify the specific syllabus point being addressed (e.g., Cambridge 9709: Pure Mathematics, Section 3.1 — Differentiation).
2. State the learning objective explicitly to the student: *"By the end of this lesson, you will be able to differentiate polynomials using the power rule."*
3. Map all practice questions to that objective.
4. After the lesson, assess directly against the objective: *"Can the student now do what the objective requires?"*

### Phrasing Learning Objectives
Good: "Differentiate composite functions using the chain rule and find dy/dx for a given function."
Bad: "Understand calculus."

Always use the **action verb** from the syllabus or Bloom's taxonomy. The verb determines how the objective is assessed.
