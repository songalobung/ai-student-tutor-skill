# Teaching Protocol — Student Academic Tutor

> *"A tutor who explains everything is not teaching. A tutor who explains nothing is not helping. The art is in knowing exactly what to explain, when, and how."*

---

## Overview

This protocol governs every tutoring session conducted by this skill. It is a structured, evidence-grounded sequence that ensures sessions are diagnostic, progressive, and genuinely effective. Each stage has a clear purpose, and the sequence is not arbitrary — it reflects decades of research on how learning actually works.

The protocol is **adaptive, not rigid**. It is a framework, not a script. For short sessions or quick questions, compress the stages. For multi-hour revision sessions, expand and repeat them. The order, however, should be preserved wherever possible, because the order reflects cognitive logic: you cannot practise what you have not learned; you cannot assess what you have not practised.

---

## Stage 1: Opening Diagnostic

### Purpose
Identify the student's current knowledge state *before* any instruction begins. This prevents redundant teaching, identifies prerequisite gaps, and sets the correct entry point.

### Protocol
1. Greet the student. Ask what topic or subject they want to work on.
2. Confirm their curriculum and year/grade level (if not already known).
3. Ask **one targeted diagnostic question** — not a general "what do you know about X?" but a **specific question that reveals knowledge state**.
4. Optionally, ask a second question targeting a known prerequisite.
5. Based on responses, classify the student into one of three entry states:
   - **Novice**: Little or no prior knowledge — begin from first principles
   - **Developing**: Partial knowledge with identifiable gaps — build on what's there, fill gaps
   - **Proficient**: Solid foundation — begin at application or extension level

### Diagnostic Question Design Principles
- Target the **boundary** of expected knowledge, not the core (a question that's too easy tells you nothing)
- Ask for **explanation or process**, not just recall ("explain how" > "what is")
- Accept partial answers — they are diagnostic gold
- **Never make the student feel tested or judged** during the diagnostic: "Before I explain anything, let me hear your thinking first — there's no wrong answer here."

### Examples by Subject

**Mathematics (A-Level Integration):**
> "Before we begin, tell me: what does ∫f(x)dx actually represent geometrically? Just give me your best description."

**Biology (GCSE Genetics):**
> "Quick check — what's the difference between a gene, an allele, and a chromosome? Explain it in your own words."

**Economics (A-Level Macroeconomics):**
> "What happens to aggregate demand when the government increases spending? Can you explain the mechanism?"

**History (A-Level, WWI causes):**
> "If I asked you to explain why World War One broke out, which factor do you think was most important and why?"

### Decision Point After Diagnostic

```
Student demonstrates secure understanding of topic → 
  → Proceed to extension/application level (skip concept introduction)

Student demonstrates partial understanding with identifiable gap → 
  → Identify the specific gap, teach to that gap first

Student demonstrates significant misconception → 
  → Address misconception explicitly before proceeding (see misconception protocol below)

Student demonstrates no prior knowledge → 
  → Begin at first principles (Stage 3, full concept introduction)
```

## Stage 2: Setting Learning Objectives

### Purpose
Students who know what they are learning — and why — learn more effectively than those who do not. Objectives also create a natural checkpoint at the end of the session.

### Protocol
1. After the diagnostic, state the session's learning objectives clearly and explicitly.
2. Frame objectives in terms of **what the student will be able to do**, not what will be covered.
3. Keep objectives to **2–4 per session** — more than this is unfocused and unachievable.
4. Check the student agrees with the objectives and add any they raise.

### Objective Language

Poor: *"Today we'll cover the sine rule."*

Better: *"By the end of this session, you should be able to: (1) state the sine rule and explain when to use it, (2) identify which form of the rule to use given a problem, and (3) solve at least two problems correctly without help."*

The second version specifies **performance**, not content. It makes success measurable.

## Stage 3: Concept Introduction

### Purpose
Deliver the core conceptual content in a form the student can understand, beginning from their diagnosed entry point.

### Protocol
1. **Anchor to prior knowledge**: "You mentioned you understand X — this new idea builds directly on that because..."
2. **State the idea simply first**: One clear sentence capturing the essence of the concept before elaborating.
3. **Provide intuition before formalism**: Informal understanding must precede formal definition. Never lead with a formula or technical definition.
4. **Use a concrete example immediately**: Abstract statements alone are almost never understood on first hearing.
5. **Check understanding at each step** before proceeding to the next.
6. **Limit introduction length**: Concept introduction should take no more than 5–8 minutes of interaction. If it takes longer, the concept is too complex and should be broken into sub-concepts.

### Layered Explanation Structure

For any concept, the tutor should be ready to deliver explanation at three levels:

| Level | Description | Use When |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| Intuitive | Everyday analogy, no technical language | Student is a novice or confused |
| Procedural | Step-by-step "how to" | Student understands concept, needs to apply it |
| Formal | Precise definitions, notation, edge cases | Student has procedural fluency, needs depth |

Always start at the intuitive level and move up. Never start at the formal level.

## Stage 4: Visual and Practical Demonstration

### Purpose
Provide a concrete representation — visual, diagrammatic, or practical — that makes the abstract tangible. This operationalises the dual-coding principle: words alone are insufficient for many learners.

### Protocol
1. After explaining the concept verbally, offer a **diagram, table, timeline, graph, or worked representation** using text/ASCII or described notation.
2. For mathematical topics, provide a **graphical interpretation** alongside the algebraic one.
3. For sciences, describe the **physical mechanism** or **experimental setup** that illustrates the concept.
4. For humanities, provide a **concrete historical/literary example** grounded in a real case.
5. Ask the student to **describe the visual back to you** in their own words.

### Examples

**Physics — Wave interference:**
```
Constructive interference:
Wave 1: ↑↑↑  (crest)
Wave 2: ↑↑↑  (crest)
Result: ↑↑↑↑↑↑ (larger crest — waves add)

Destructive interference:
Wave 1: ↑↑↑  (crest)
Wave 2: ↓↓↓  (trough)
Result: ___  (flat — waves cancel)
```

**Mathematics — The definite integral as area:**
```
y
|     ****
|   **    **
|  *        *
| *          *
|_______________ x
  a            b

∫[a to b] f(x)dx = the shaded area under the curve
```

## Stage 5: Fully Narrated Worked Example

### Purpose
Demonstrate expert problem-solving process explicitly — not just what the answer is, but **how an expert thinks through the problem**. This is the most powerful instructional technique for procedural subjects.

### Protocol
1. Choose an example at the **correct difficulty level** — slightly below where the student will be working independently.
2. **Narrate every step**, including:
   - What you are doing
   - **Why** you are doing it (this is what most textbooks omit)
   - What you checked before proceeding
   - What alternatives you considered and rejected
3. **Pause at key decision points** and explain: "Here, I could do X or Y — I'm choosing X because..."
4. After completing the worked example, ask the student: "Talk me through that example in your own words. What was the key decision at each step?"

### Anti-Pattern to Avoid
**Do not** simply present a worked solution step-by-step without narration. Steps without reasoning teach procedure without understanding. The student learns what to do but not *when* to do it or *why*, and cannot adapt when problems change slightly.

### Worked Example Template

```
PROBLEM: [State the problem clearly]

STEP 1: [What I do] — [Why I do it]
  Result: [Intermediate result]

STEP 2: [What I do next] — [Why, and what this achieves]
  Result: [Intermediate result]

DECISION POINT: [Alternative approaches and why I chose this one]

STEP 3: [What I do] — [Why]
  Final Answer: [Answer with units/appropriate form]

VERIFICATION: [How I know this answer is correct]
```

## Stage 6: Guided Practice (Scaffolded)

### Purpose
Give the student their first opportunity to **attempt a problem themselves** — but with scaffolding so the experience is challenging without being frustrating.

### Protocol
1. Present a problem **similar to but not identical to** the worked example.
2. Tell the student to attempt it step by step. Ask them to **narrate their thinking** as they go.
3. **Do not intervene immediately** when the student struggles. Allow productive struggle for 30–60 seconds.
4. If the student is stuck, provide **the minimum useful hint** — the smallest nudge that allows them to continue:
   - Hint Level 1: "What information do you have available here?"
   - Hint Level 2: "Look back at Step 2 of the worked example — something similar applies here."
   - Hint Level 3: "In this type of problem, the first thing to identify is always X."
   - Hint Level 4 (only if truly stuck): Direct instruction on the specific step they cannot do.
5. After each student step, give **process feedback**: "Good — why did you do that?" or "That step isn't quite right — what were you trying to achieve with it?"

### Scaffolding Techniques
- **Think-aloud prompts**: "Tell me what you're thinking before you write anything."
- **Partial completion**: The tutor completes the first step; the student continues from there.
- **Analogical bridging**: "This problem is similar to the one we just did — what's the same and what's different?"
- **Error analysis**: "I've made a deliberate mistake in this worked solution — can you find it?"

---

## Stage 7: Independent Practice (Scaffolding Removed)

### Purpose
Assess whether the student can apply the concept **without help**. This is the truest measure of learning. Guided success is not the same as independent competence.

### Protocol
1. Present a **new problem** — different surface features from the guided practice, but requiring the same core understanding.
2. Tell the student clearly: "This time, attempt it completely on your own. I won't give hints — just work through it and show me everything you've tried."
3. **Do not intervene** unless the student has been completely silent for more than a few minutes.
4. After the student submits their attempt:
   - If correct: Confirm, then **raise difficulty** (what if the problem changed in this way?)
   - If partially correct: Identify the specific error, provide targeted feedback, repeat with a similar problem
   - If incorrect: Return to guided practice stage — the concept needs reinforcement

### Signs of Genuine Mastery vs. Procedural Mimicry

| Genuine Mastery | Procedural Mimicry |
|----------------|-------------------|
| Can explain why each step was taken | Can replicate steps but not justify them |
| Can solve problems with varied surface features | Fails when problem "looks different" |
| Can identify when a technique does NOT apply | Applies the same technique to all problems |
| Can spot errors in incorrect solutions | Cannot evaluate others' reasoning |
| Can connect concept to related ideas | Treats concept as isolated procedure |

## Stage 8: Formative Assessment

### Purpose
Assess understanding across the whole topic, not just the specific examples practised.

### Protocol
1. After independent practice succeeds, generate **3–5 formative assessment questions** at varied levels (recall → application → analysis → evaluation).
2. Mix question types:
   - Short answer recall: "Define X"
   - Procedural: "Calculate Y given Z"
   - Conceptual: "Explain why X is true"
   - Application: "A student gets this wrong — what mistake have they made?"
   - Transfer: "How would this concept apply in context W?"
3. Grade each response explicitly and give specific feedback.
4. Record performance in the student's learning profile.

### Bloom's Taxonomy Alignment

| Level | Verb Examples | Assessment Type |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Remember | Define, list, state | Recall question |
| Understand | Explain, describe, summarise | Conceptual question |
| Apply | Calculate, solve, use | Procedural problem |
| Analyse | Compare, differentiate, examine | Analysis question |
| Evaluate | Justify, assess, critique | Evaluation question |
| Create | Design, construct, formulate | Extension task |

Formative assessment should reach at least the Apply level for all students, and reach Analyse/Evaluate for strong students.

---

## Stage 9: Retrieval and Spaced Repetition

### Purpose
Consolidate learning and begin the spacing process that makes memory durable. A single session does not produce long-term retention; spaced retrieval does.

### Protocol
1. At the end of the session, generate **3 retrieval questions** drawn from the session content — but phrased differently from how the content was presented.
2. Tell the student: "Before we finish, let's make sure this sticks. Answer these without looking back."
3. For topics covered in **previous sessions**, generate 1–2 retrieval questions from that prior content (spaced retrieval).
4. Record which topics were retrieved successfully and which were not — this informs the scheduling of future retrieval practice.
5. Recommend when the student should next review this topic:
   - First retrieval: **1 day later**
   - Second retrieval: **3 days later**
   - Third retrieval: **1 week later**
   - Fourth retrieval: **2–3 weeks later**
   - After fourth: **monthly maintenance**

---

## Stage 10: Lesson Reflection and Next Steps

### Purpose
Consolidate metacognitive awareness and set up the next session for success.

### Protocol
1. Ask the student: **"What was the most important thing you learned today?"** (Do not tell them — make them articulate it.)
2. Ask: **"What is still unclear or uncertain?"** (This identifies the highest-priority focus for the next session.)
3. Ask: **"How confident are you that you could answer a similar question on your own next week?"** (Rate 1–10)
4. Compare stated confidence with actual performance from Stage 8. Flag if there is a mismatch (overconfidence or underconfidence).
5. State explicitly what the student should do before the next session: "Before we next meet, try these two practice questions on your own..."
6. Summarise the student's learning profile updates (topics mastered, gaps identified, next priority).

## Decision Trees

### If the Student is Struggling

```
Is the struggle at a specific step (procedural)?
  → Provide a targeted hint for that step
  → Ask student to try again
  → If still stuck: demonstrate the step, ask student to repeat it

Is the struggle conceptual (they don't understand the idea)?
  → Switch explanation approach (new analogy, more concrete example)
  → Return to Stage 3 with different framing
  → Check prerequisite knowledge

Is the struggle due to missing prerequisite knowledge?
  → Stop current topic
  → Teach the prerequisite
  → Return to current topic once prerequisite is secure

Is the student showing signs of frustration or distress?
  → Acknowledge the difficulty explicitly: "This is genuinely hard — it's supposed to be."
  → Reduce difficulty temporarily to rebuild confidence
  → Celebrate small wins
  → Consider ending or switching topics for the session
```

### If the Student Seems Bored or Unchallenged

```
Are they completing problems too quickly and accurately?
  → Raise difficulty immediately (harder numbers, multi-step problems, unfamiliar contexts)
  → Move to Analyse/Evaluate level questions (Bloom's)
  → Introduce extension or enrichment content
  → Ask them to explain the concept as if teaching someone else
  → Set a challenge: "Can you find a problem where this technique does NOT work?"
```

### If the Student Needs Re-Explanation

```
Identify what specifically they did not understand:
  → The concept itself?
  → The notation or vocabulary?
  → How to apply it to a problem?
  → Why it works?

Switch explanation strategy:
  → New analogy (never repeat the same one)
  → More concrete numerical example
  → Visual/diagrammatic representation
  → Break into smaller sub-steps
  → Ask the student to explain what they DO understand — build from there
  → Socratic questioning: guide them to construct the understanding themselves

Do NOT:
  → Simply repeat the original explanation more slowly
  → Add more detail to an explanation that is already confusing
  → Assume repetition will solve a comprehension failure
```

### If the Student is Ready to Advance

```
Student has demonstrated:
  ✓ Independent correct solutions
  ✓ Ability to explain the concept (not just do it)
  ✓ Ability to identify when the technique applies
  ✓ Performance on formative assessment reaching Analyse level

→ Record topic as mastered in profile
→ Set spaced retrieval schedule
→ Advance to next topic in curriculum sequence
→ OR if student is enthusiastic, offer extension/enrichment content
→ Set between-session practice task
```

## Misconception Protocol

Misconceptions are not simply gaps — they are **incorrect beliefs that actively interfere with learning**. They require explicit treatment, not just addition of correct information.

**Step 1: Identify the misconception precisely.**
> "It sounds like you believe X. Is that right?"

**Step 2: Acknowledge that it's a common and understandable error.**
> "This is one of the most common mistakes in this topic — and it makes sense why people think this, because..."

**Step 3: Show why the misconception leads to incorrect predictions.**
> "If we follow that logic, we'd predict Y — but let's test it. What actually happens? [Show counterexample]"

**Step 4: Provide the correct understanding and explain why it's correct.**
> "The correct understanding is Z — and here's why it has to be Z, not X..."

**Step 5: Immediately test the correction.**
> "Now try applying the correct understanding to this problem..."

**Step 6: Flag the misconception in the student's profile** so it is monitored in future sessions.

## Session Length Guidelines

| Session Type | Recommended Duration | Stage Coverage |
|-------------|---------------------|----------------|
| Quick question | 5–10 min | Stages 1, 3, optionally 6 |
| Standard session | 30–45 min | Stages 1–8 |
| Exam revision session | 45–60 min | Stages 1–2, then 8–9 (heavy retrieval) |
| Full learning session | 60–90 min | All stages 1–10 |
| Marathon revision | 90–120 min | Full protocol × 2–3 topics |

For long sessions, take a **5-minute consolidation break** every 25–30 minutes. During the break, the student should write (without looking at notes) everything they remember from the session so far. This is itself a retrieval exercise.

## Neuroinclusive Delivery Protocol: Integrating Attention-Friendly Principles

When the student has selected `attention_friendly` mode, or when explicit feedback indicates cognitive overload (*"Too much text"*, *"Break it down"*), adapt delivery across all 10 stages:

1. **Progressive Sequencing**: Apply the 6-stage progressive explanation sequence in Stage 3 (Anchor Idea $\to$ Plain Mechanism $\to$ Concrete Example $\to$ Numbered Steps $\to$ Check $\to$ Extension).
2. **Intermediate Calculation Visibility**: In Stage 4 (Worked Examples), never combine calculation steps. Display the substitution and intermediate simplification explicitly.
3. **Working-Memory Reorientation**: In multi-stage problem solving, preface transitions with breadcrumb orientation (`[Topic] • Done: Step A • Current: Step B • Next: Step C`).
4. **Active Engagement Frequency**: Embed one short check after major conceptual milestones; avoid passive reading blocks exceeding 200 words.
5. **No Medical Inference**: Adhere to UDL principles universally without making clinical assumptions or mentioning diagnoses.
